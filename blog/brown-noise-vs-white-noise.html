Brown Noise vs White Noise: Which Is Better for Sleep, Focus, and Babies?

By Jeremy Stakich · September 27, 2026 · Sleep · Sounds · Focus

Quick answer: Brown noise is a deep, soft rumble, like a waterfall or strong wind, while white noise is a brighter, even hiss, like TV static or a fan. Brown noise is often the more soothing choice for sleep and sound-sensitive listeners, and white noise is better at covering voices and sharp sounds for studying or light sleepers. Try both at a low volume and keep the one your body relaxes into.

Brown noise vs white noise is one of the most common sound questions for sleep, studying, babies, and sensory needs. Both are steady, random sounds that cover distracting noise, but they feel very different in your ears. This guide explains the difference in plain words, which one fits which situation, a simple test to choose, safe volume tips, and how to try both on your iPhone or iPad.

Brown noise vs white noise: the difference in plain words

White noise

  • Sounds like: TV static, a fan, an air purifier, or a steady “shhh”
  • What it is: equal energy across all frequencies, so the high end sounds bright and hissy
  • Best at: covering voices, clicks, and sharp sounds

Brown noise

  • Sounds like: a waterfall, heavy rain on a roof, strong wind, or a low airplane cabin hum
  • What it is: most of the energy is in the low frequencies, so it sounds deep and soft
  • Best at: a warm, gentle background that feels less sharp on the ears

A quick way to picture it: white noise is bright and crisp, brown noise is dark and cozy. Pink noise sits in between—if you are curious about that one, see brown noise vs pink noise and our guide to every noise color.

Brown noise vs white noise for sleep

Steady sound helps sleep mainly by covering the bumps that wake you: traffic, a partner snoring, a dog barking, or a door closing. In a small sleep-lab study of 18 healthy adults, steady broadband sound played through room speakers helped people fall asleep about 38% faster in a model of trouble falling asleep (Messineo et al., Frontiers in Neurology).

  • Choose brown noise if you want a soft, low sound that feels relaxing and does not hiss.
  • Choose white noise if you are a light sleeper in a noisy place and need stronger masking of voices or street sounds.

Many people start with brown for comfort and switch to white on noisy nights. To keep sound playing all night with the screen off, see Lock Screen background sounds for sleep.

Brown noise vs white noise for studying and focus

For focus, steady noise gives your brain a predictable background so sudden sounds stand out less. A 2024 review of 13 studies with 335 young people found that white and pink noise gave a small boost on attention tasks for children and young adults with ADHD or high attention difficulties (Nigg et al., Journal of the American Academy of Child & Adolescent Psychiatry).

  • White noise: great for noisy study spaces—cafes, shared rooms, open offices—where voices are the main distraction.
  • Brown noise: popular for long study sessions because the low rumble is easy to forget about. Those studies tested white and pink noise, so choosing brown comes down to comfort.

Brown noise vs white noise for babies

Many parents use steady sound to cover household noise during naps and at night. Babies often settle with a soft “shhh” similar to white noise, while brown noise can feel gentler in a quiet nursery.

  • Keep the volume low—softer than normal conversation.
  • Place the phone or speaker across the room, never in the crib.
  • Use a steady sound without sudden changes, music, or voices.
  • Test both for a few naps and keep the one your baby settles with best.

Brown noise vs white noise for sensory overload

For kids and adults who are sensitive to sound, brown noise is often the easier place to start because its lower, smoother sound has less of the bright hiss that can feel scratchy. White noise can still help when the problem is chatter or sharp noises nearby. The National Autistic Society notes that moving to a quieter space and using calming items from a sensory kit can help when input gets too much (National Autistic Society: sensory processing)—a familiar steady sound in headphones can be one of those tools.

For in-the-moment help with a child, see how to help an overstimulated child calm down.

How to choose: a 3-step listening test

  1. Set a low volume. Start quieter than you think—just enough to soften room noise.
  2. Try each for five minutes. Play white noise, then brown noise, in the place you will actually use it.
  3. Notice your body. Which one made your shoulders drop and your breathing slow? Which one did you stop noticing? Keep that one.

Can’t decide? Mix them. A layer of brown for warmth with a little white for masking is a favorite for many listeners. Our guide on how to mix color noise walks through it.

Safe listening volume

The National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders explains that sounds at or below 70 decibels are unlikely to cause hearing loss, while long or repeated exposure at 85 decibels and above can (NIDCD: noise-induced hearing loss). Normal conversation is about 60–70 decibels, so aim for background sound that is softer than talking, especially overnight, with headphones, and for children.

FAQ

Is brown noise or white noise better for sleep?

Brown noise is often more relaxing because it is deeper and softer, while white noise is better at covering voices and street sounds. Try each at a low volume for a few nights and keep the one you sleep best with.

Is brown noise better than white noise for studying?

Both can help. White noise covers chatter well in noisy places, and brown noise is easy to tune out during long sessions. Pick the one that lets you forget the sound is there.

Is brown noise or white noise better for babies?

Many babies settle with either. Keep the volume softer than conversation, place the device across the room, and use a steady sound without music or voices.

Can I mix brown noise and white noise?

Yes. Mixing a warm brown layer with a little white noise gives comfort and stronger masking together. In Stress Free Flow, My Favorite Sound lets you save a custom mix with a slider for each color.

The takeaway

The brown noise vs white noise choice comes down to comfort and your space: brown noise for a deep, soothing background, white noise for crisp masking of voices and sharp sounds. Keep the volume low, test both where you will use them, and mix them if you want the best of each.
